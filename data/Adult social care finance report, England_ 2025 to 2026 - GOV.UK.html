    Issue affecting Nottinghamshire’s 2025 to 2026 adult social care finance return data

    We are aware of errors affecting Nottinghamshire’s data in the adult social care finance return (ASC-FR) 2025 to 2026.

    The full extent of the errors is not currently known and is still under investigation. Users should therefore treat Nottinghamshire’s 2025 to 2026 ASC-FR figures with caution. Until corrected data are available and published here, users requiring figures for Nottinghamshire may wish to refer to its 2024 to 2025 ASC-FR data or its 2025 to 2026 Revenue Outturn (RO3) return, depending on their purpose.

    The errors will affect some England-level totals. Using Nottinghamshire’s 2024 to 2025 figures as an indication of the potential scale, for example income from the NHS could be around 4% higher at England level than currently published. The equivalent impact on other headline measures, including client contributions, net current expenditure, gross current expenditure and total public spending, is likely less than 1%. The actual impact will not be known until corrected data are available.

    We are working with Nottinghamshire to correct the data and expect to publish updated figures in the first quarter of 2027. We will update this publication once corrected data are available.

    If you have any queries, please contact us at ascfr@dhsc.gov.uk.

    Introduction

    This report presents data on adult social care finances in England for the period 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026. All references to years relate to those running 1 April to 31 March. It draws on data from the adult social care finance return (ASC-FR) submitted by each local authority. It provides information on how much local authorities spent on adult social care and the sources of funding. All figures in this report are presented in cash terms unless stated otherwise. Note: some figures in this report may not sum due to rounding.

    Prior to 2024 to 2025, this finance report was previously published as the Adult social care activity and finance report by NHS England.

    The Department of Health and Social Care also publishes the Adult social care activity report, England: 2025 to 2026.

    How the data can be used

    This report may interest members of the public, policy officials and other stakeholders. It can be used to compare local and national data, and monitor the quality and effectiveness of services.

    You can:

    • use this report to consider similarities and differences in the trends shown in the data. For example, if expenditure has increased or decreased for a type of social care, check the related activity trends in the activity report to understand the full picture
    • use this data to increase your understanding of how local authorities commission and deliver social care
    • use the data quality outputs to help you understand variation and change at local level. Some changes may reflect operational practice, methodological changes or how a local authority responded to wider economic shocks, rather than a change in demand
    • contact us if you have any questions about the data published - see the ‘Contact’ section below

    You cannot:

    • divide expenditure by activity data to work out a cost per person. For example, the client level data (CLD) used in the activity return does not distinguish between someone receiving one week of care and someone receiving long-term support for a full year
    • use this data to judge performance as good or bad
    • directly compare long-term activity data with the long-term finance data published here. The 2 reports define long-term care differently. For example, activity data includes clients receiving long-term care with a primary support reason (PSR) of social support. In finance data, this PSR is not included in long-term expenditure but recorded as a combined short-term and long-term spend. Because of these definitional differences, you must not compare the 2 data sets directly

    Note on the impacts of COVID-19

    There have been no specific funding measures in place to address the residual impact of the COVID-19 pandemic since financial year 2021 to 2022. However, these were in place in previous years (2020 to 2021 and 2021 to 2022) so this should be considered when looking at longer-term trends.

    Main points

    The main points outlined in this report are that:

    • gross current expenditure (GCE) on adult social care by local authorities was £31.6 billion in 2025 to 2026. This was £2.3 billion (8%) higher than the previous year in cash terms
    • over three-quarters (81% or £25.8 billion) of total GCE was spent on long-term support in 2025 to 2026. This was £2.2 billion (9%) higher than in 2024 to 2025
    • income from client contributions increased by 6% (£0.3 billion) between 2024 to 2025 and 2025 to 2026. It accounted for the highest share of local authority income, staying ahead of income from the NHS (which decreased by 1%). Overall income increased by 2% to £9.8 billion
    • the average cost of a week of residential or nursing care increased by 6% (£65.53) to £1,251.09. The average cost of an hour of externally provided home care was £24.72, an increase of 5% (£1.16) on 2024 to 2025

    Gross current expenditure

    GCE is the amount local authorities spend after subtracting capital charges and income from all sources except client contributions. During the 2025 to 2026 financial year, GCE on adult social care was £31.6 billion. It is a leading measure of local government spending. Unless stated otherwise, figures in this report use GCE.

    It is important to be aware of the parts that contribute to calculating GCE, as shown below for 2025 to 2026. Any year-on-year changes to GCE will be driven by increases or decreases in one or more of these areas.

    The calculation is shown below.

    Gross current expenditure (£31,644 million) equals total expenditure (£36,710 million) minus:

    • £177 million capital charges
    • £122 million from joint arrangements

    • £438 million from ‘other’ income

    Net current expenditure

    NCE is calculated by subtracting client contributions from GCE. This is also known as local authority spending.

    NCE is useful for understanding how much of adult social care is funded solely from local authorities. This includes money raised locally such as Council Tax (including the adult social care precept) and business rates. This also includes money from central government, such as the local government finance settlement. NCE does not include capital charges or external income. It is unaffected by variations in client contributions or income from the NHS, which are the 2 largest income sources.

    Due to changing government policy, responsibility and funding for providing certain adult social care services often shifts between different public bodies, most commonly between the NHS and local authorities. This means that it is not always meaningful to compare expenditure on adult social care over time if based only on expenditure by social services departments.

    To calculate overall public spending (OPS), planned spending on the minimum BCF and direct spending with social care providers are added to the figure for NCE. OPS for 2025 to 2026 is estimated at £30 billion, a 7.8% (£2.2 billion) increase in cash terms or a 4.1% (£1.2 billion) increase in real terms from 2024 to 2025.

    Valuing people now

    The valuing people now initiative aimed to give greater independence to young adults with learning disabilities. Responsibility for providing services to this cohort lay with the NHS before being transferred to local authorities in 2008 to 2009. Until 2011 to 2012, funding continued to be provided through the NHS and so was not included in the net expenditure by social services departments. From 2011 to 2012, funding for this initiative was allocated directly to local authorities and therefore is included in the net expenditure of social services departments. The actual value of the transfer is known for 2010 to 2011 only and has been assumed to be flat in real terms (adjusted using the GDP deflator as shown in the section ‘Use of GDP deflator’) to provide a figure for previous years.

    NHS transfer to local authorities

    As per the Secretary of State directions 2014, these monies were transferred from the NHS to local authorities for the provision of social care services that also benefit health. As they are considered to be income, the sums are not included in the net expenditure reported by social services departments.

    BCF

    BCF was introduced in 2015 to 2016. This figure specifically relates to planned spend from the minimum contribution made by clinical commissioning groups (which were replaced by integrated care boards in 2022) to the total BCF joint fund pool. This figure constitutes jointly agreed planned expenditure classified as ‘social care’ in BCF plans (all commissioner types and all provider types) and planned expenditure that was not classified as ‘social care’ but was commissioned from non-NHS providers (local authority, charity and/or voluntary sector and private sector - by all commissioner types).

    Winter pressures transfer

    As per commitments made in 2012, these monies were transferred from the NHS to local authorities for the provision of social care services that also benefit health, specifically over the winter period. As they are considered to be income, the sums are not included in the net expenditure reported by social services departments.

    3. Long-term care

    Long-term care refers to ongoing support provided with the intention of maintaining quality of life for an individual. This support ranges from intensive services, such as nursing care, to lower-level assistance within the community. Examples include direct payments for regular home care visits. Long-term care is provided according to assessed needs and continues for as long as the individual requires support.

    All figures in this section are presented in cash terms unless stated otherwise.

    Main points on long-term care

    In the 2025 to 2026 financial year, 81% of total GCE (£25.8 billion) was spent on long-term care. This includes residential, nursing and community care.

    This was an increase of £2.2 billion (9%) compared with 2024 to 2025.

    As demonstrated in figure 4 below, spending on long-term care was split almost equally between age groups:

    • spending of £12.6 billion (49%) on clients aged 18 to 64

    4. Short-term care

    Following a request for support and an assessment, clients may be provided with a period of short-term care.

    Short-term care falls into 2 categories:

    • short-term support to maximise independence (ST-Max): this includes all episodes of support provided that aim to be time limited, with the intention of maximising the independence of the individual and reducing and/or eliminating their need for ongoing support by the local authorities

    7. Unit costs

    What a unit cost is

    A unit cost is the average cost of providing a set amount of services. For long-term care, unit costs are calculated on a per week, per person basis, where unit cost equals total expenditure, minus grants to voluntary organisations, divided by total activity (in weeks).

    Note: unlike other figures in this report, unit costs are based on the total expenditure minus grants to voluntary organisations, not GCE. No income is excluded from the figure.

    Nursing costs are expected to be reported exclusive of funded nursing care contributions.

    The activity data used in the unit cost calculation is collected as part of the ASC-FR. Local authorities provide the number of weeks of care provided, where activity (weeks) equals hours of care provided in week, divided by total hours in the week, multiplied by the number of weeks for which care was provided.

    The total hours in a week is based on a 24-hour day and therefore 168 hours in a week.

    All figures in this section are presented in cash terms unless stated otherwise.

    Terminology

    Better Care Fund (BCF)

    BCF is a single pooled budget shared by the NHS and local government. It is designed to incentivise closer joint working, with a focus on improving clients’ wellbeing.

    More details can be found on Local Government Association’s Better Care Fund Support Programme webpage.

    Cash terms

    This refers to the nominal value of money without adjusting for inflation. It represents the actual amount of money spent or received.

    Gross current expenditure (GCE)

    Total expenditure minus capital charges and all income sources, except client contributions.

    Gross current public expenditure

    Net current expenditure, plus income from the NHS.

    Gross total expenditure

    All spending by local authorities, including capital charges and client contributions, but excluding income from joint arrangements, NHS and other income.

    Local authorities

    This report refers to local authorities throughout instead of using ‘councils with adult social services responsibilities’.

    Local Authority Better Care Grant

    The LABCG is a grant that provides local government with additional funding for adult social care and support for hospital discharge. It is paid directly to local authorities and must be used to support social care activity. In 2025 to 2026, the LABCG replaced the iBCF and the local authority element of the Discharge Fund.

    Long-term care

    Any service or support that is provided with the intention of maintaining quality of life for an individual on an ongoing basis.

    Net current expenditure (NCE - also known as local authority spend)

    Total expenditure minus capital charges and all income sources.

    Net total expenditure

    Total expenditure minus all income sources, plus capital charges.

    Other short-term support

    All other short-term services - for example, emergency support - that are not otherwise included in short-term support to maximise independence.

    Real terms

    This refers to the value of money after adjusting for inflation. It provides a more accurate representation of the purchasing power of money over time by removing the effects of inflation. In this report, the GDP deflator is used to make this adjustment. See the ‘Use of GDP deflator’ section below for more detail.

    Short-term care

    All or any episodes of support provided that are intended to be time limited.

    Short-term support to maximise independence (ST-Max)

    This includes all episodes of support provided that are intended to be time limited, with the intention of maximising the independence of the individual and reducing and/or eliminating their need for ongoing support by the local authority.

    Total expenditure

    All spending by local authorities including capital charges.

    Purpose

    This section constitutes a background data quality report. For more specific information about the quality of the latest year’s data, see the ‘adult-social-care-finance-report-2025-2026-data-quality-report’ ODS file in the ‘Adult social care finance report, England: 2025 to 2026 - data pack’, which is published alongside this report.

    This data quality statement will assess the quality of the statistics produced from ASC-FR, which local authorities in England submit to DHSC.

    ASC-FR covers adults, defined as those aged 18 or over, and relates to adult social care services. Children’s social services are not covered in this return, nor are services provided to adults on behalf of children (for example, section 17 payments). Some local authorities are known to continue with children’s services for a few clients aged 18 or over - these clients should be included within the adult return.     Overview

    Through the respective collections’ working groups, DHSC works with local authorities and other stakeholders to continually develop the data template and the accompanying guidance documents to improve clarity and ease of completion.

    Note: the 2021 English Census is the most current Office for National Statistics (ONS) population data available at the disaggregation required for this report.

    Long-term care

    • revaluation losses, asset disposals and changes in accounting treatment affected some long-term support figures

    Accuracy and reliability

    The accuracy of ASC-FR data is the responsibility of the local authorities that submit the data to DHSC. ASC-FR is an aggregate collection taken from administrative systems. As DHSC does not have access to the individual records behind the aggregate counts, we are reliant on local authorities to assess their own data quality. However, we aim to assist in this process by providing a bespoke data quality report (see point 3 below).

    In many instances, assessing reliability depends on local knowledge as each local authority determines the approach taken in their area. What may be an anomaly in one area could be considered standard practice elsewhere. However, a range of activities are undertaken (outlined in more detail below) to check and improve data quality. Discussions with local authorities have provided useful anecdotal information about distributions and trends.

    DHSC performs validation checks within the data returns and provides post-submission data quality reports for each local authority to identify both:

    • logical inconsistencies in the data
    • where the data submitted is an outlier against either local and national data or against submissions from previous years

    Local authorities are then able to review and resubmit data ahead of the deadline or provide explanations for any identified issues.

    The submission and validation process for each collection is carried out as follows.

    1. Local authority collates the data for submission in the relevant collection form. This form includes inbuilt validations to allow local authorities to check their data for common issues prior to submission.
    2. Local authority submits data by the mandated deadline.
    3. All local authorities that have met this deadline receive a data quality report covering critical validations and providing some derived totals, allowing local authorities to confirm their data is correct.
    4. DHSC reviews the quality of all files submitted and may provide additional support to local authorities with substantial data quality issues.
    5. Local authorities can resubmit data to amend any identified quality issues.
    6. Final deadline for submission.
    7. Following the final deadline, DHSC carries out analysis of the quality of final data. Although local authorities cannot resubmit data after this point, they may be contacted for additional clarification or context.

    The validation checks carried out throughout this process include:

    • validations across worksheets to check figures across different tables that store the same data
    • checks for missing data items
    • checks for instances of recorded activity without associated expenditure or associated expenditure without recorded activity
    • comparing the unit costs with those from other local authorities and flagging outliers

    ASC-FR contains complex elements and this, combined with the aggregate nature of the collections, means that some data quality issues are not always immediately apparent. Furthermore, the annual nature of the collections means that any issues with the submitted data can sometimes take a while to be identified and worked through with local authorities.

    More detailed queries can be found in the ‘Data quality summary’ sheet of the data quality report ODS file, which can be found in the accompanying ‘Adult social care finance report, England: 2025 to 2026 - data pack’.

    A common issue raised by local authorities in previous years is that allocating expenditure by PSR in the ASC-FR can be challenging, as many finance systems are not configured to collect this data.

    Information on the number of local authorities returning data at the mandated and final deadlines across both collections is available in the ‘Submission status report’ sheet of the data quality report ODS file in the accompanying data pack.

Methods of disclosure control are discussed and the most appropriate methods implemented. As a result of this process, finance data is unsuppressed.

    DHSC aims to be transparent in all its activities. A description of the collection process and any issues with the quality of the 2025 to 2026 ASC-FR data are documented in the accompanying ODS data quality report within the ‘Adult social care finance report, England: 2025 to 2026 - data pack’.

    ASC-FR data is submitted to DHSC through a secure electronic file transfer system called the Strategic Data Collection Service (SDCS), which is hosted by NHS England. The submitted files are transferred from SDCS and stored on a secure network with restricted access folders.

    NHS England publishes information on how it complies with the Code of Practice for Official Statistics, including details on its confidentiality policy, a user engagement statement and a corrections policy. Many of these apply jointly to both NHS England and DHSC.

    Use of GDP deflator

    When looking at changes in monetary amounts over time it can be difficult to see whether more or less money has been spent as the real change is often masked by the effects of inflation. Therefore, it is useful to strip out the effects of inflation so the real change in expenditure can be examined - and one way to do this is through use of a deflator.

    In this report, the GDP deflator is used. The GDP deflator is a much broader price index than the Consumer Prices Index (CPI), Retail Prices Index (RPI) or Retail Prices Index excluding mortgage interest payments (RPIX), which only measure consumer prices. This is because it reflects the prices of all domestically produced goods and services in the economy. The GDP deflator also includes the prices of investment goods, government services and exports, and excludes the price of UK imports. The wider coverage of the GDP deflator makes it more appropriate for deflating public expenditure series.

    The GDP deflator can be viewed as a measure of general inflation in the domestic economy, which can be described as a measure of price changes over time. The deflator is usually expressed in terms of an index (a time series of index numbers) and percentage changes on the previous year are also usually shown.

    It reflects movements of hundreds of separate deflators for the individual expenditure components of GDP, which include:

    • expenditure on items such as bread
    • investment in computers
    • imports of aircraft
    • exports of consultancy services

    The time series for the GDP deflator allows for the effects of changes in price (inflation) to be removed so that a time series of data - in this case, adult social care expenditure - can be expressed in ‘real’ terms.

    Example 1 - comparing with 2008 to 2009

    In 2008 to 2009, £100 was spent on a piece of equipment for a client. In 2025 to 2026, a replacement piece of equipment had to be bought for £110. In cash terms, the cost of this piece of equipment has risen by £10 (10%). However, after accounting for inflation, £100 in 2008 to 2009 was equivalent to £154.78 in 2025 to 2026 prices (see calculation below). This means that the replacement was £44.78 or 28.9% cheaper in real terms in 2025 to 2026.

    Calculation

    2008 to 2009 price in 2025 to 2026 real terms equals 2008 to 2009 price multiplied by (GDP deflator for 2025 to 2026 divided by the GDP deflator for 2008 to 2009).

    Therefore: 2008 to 2009 price in 2025 to 2026 real terms equals £100 multiplied by (100 divided by 64.6079) equals £154.78.

    The real-terms difference is £154.78 minus £110 equals £44.78. This is equivalent to a decrease of 28.9%.

    Example 2 - comparing with last year

    In 2024 to 2025, £100 was spent on a piece of equipment for a client. In 2025 to 2026, a replacement piece of equipment had to be bought for £110. In cash terms, the cost of this piece of equipment has increased by £10 (10%). After accounting for inflation, £100 in 2024 to 2025 was equivalent to £103.45 in 2025 to 2026 prices (see calculation below). This means that the replacement piece of equipment was £6.55 or 6.3% more expensive in real terms in 2025 to 2026.

    Calculation

    2024 to 2025 price in 2025 to 2026 real terms equals 2024 to 2025 price multiplied by (GDP deflator for 2025 to 2026 divided by the GDP deflator for 2024 to 2025).

    Therefore: 2024 to 2025 price in 2025 to 2026 real terms equals £100 multiplied by (100 divided by 96.6694) equals £103.45.

    The real-terms difference is £110 minus £103.45 equals £6.55. This is equivalent to an increase of 6.3%.
